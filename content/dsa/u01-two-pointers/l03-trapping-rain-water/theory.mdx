<Hook>
"Given bar heights, how much rain water is trapped after it rains?" It is a Hard, it shows up in Goldman Sachs CoderPad
write-ups, and it is usually the follow-up to Container With Most Water. The trap is to treat it like Container: here
**every bar in between matters**, and the answer is a sum over all columns, not one best pair.
</Hook>

## The picture: one column at a time

Stand on one column and look left and right. Water above you can rise until it would spill over the **lower** of the
two tallest walls around you, one on each side. So for every column `i`:

**water[i] = min(tallest bar on the left, tallest bar on the right) - height[i]**

(The "tallest on the left" includes the column itself, so the result is never negative.) The answer is the sum over all
columns. Everything else in this chapter is about computing those two maxima cheaply.

</Explain>

## Step 1: write the two maxima down (O(n) time, O(n) space)

Brute force scans both sides for every column: O(n²). Instead, sweep once from the left keeping the running maximum,
once from the right, then combine. For the example `[0, 1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 1, 3, 2, 1, 2, 1]` the real arrays are on the
board, and the water adds up to **6**.

This version is worth saying first in an interview: it is correct, easy to explain, and O(n). Then you improve the
space.

</Explain>

## Step 2: two pointers, no arrays (O(n) time, O(1) space)

You don't need both full arrays. Keep `leftMax` and `rightMax` as you walk inward from both ends, and each step
**settle the side whose bar is lower**:

- If `height[left] < height[right]`, the right side already has a wall at least as tall as `height[left]`. So for
  the left column, the *left* maximum is the one that decides its water: `leftMax - height[left]` (or a new
  `leftMax` and no water). Then `left++`.
- Otherwise do the same on the right with `rightMax`, then `right--`.

It is the Container idea again: the lower side is the one you can decide about now. Press **Next** and watch each
column fill.

</Explain>

## Why settling the lower side is safe

Say `height[left] < height[right]`. The water above `left` is `min(trueLeftMax, trueRightMax) - height[left]`.

- `trueLeftMax` is exactly our `leftMax`: we have seen everything to the left.
- `trueRightMax` is at least `leftMax`. If `leftMax` is the current bar, the right pointer stands on a taller one. If
  `leftMax` came from a bar we walked past earlier, we only walked past it because the right pointer stood on a
  **taller** bar at that moment, and that bar is still on the right side: either still under the right pointer, or
  already counted in `rightMax`.

So `min(trueLeftMax, trueRightMax)` is `leftMax`, and the left column can be settled without knowing the true right
maximum. The same holds on the right. Each step settles one column, so at most n steps.

</Explain>

## The trap: thinking it is Container With Most Water

Container only uses the two chosen walls. Here, bars in the middle can hold water **higher than the end walls**.
Using just the two end walls as the level is wrong, even though it sometimes gives the right number by luck:
on `[1, 0, 4, 0, 4, 0, 1]` it returns 3, while the real answer is 6 (the pool between the two 4s is 4 deep).

<Callout kind="trap">
Two pointers is not "the Container code with a sum". The pointer rule compares **bar heights** to pick the side, but
the water uses that side's **running max**. Mixing those up (or updating the max after adding water) gives wrong,
sometimes negative, totals.
</Callout>

</Explain>

<CheatCard title="Cheat card: Trapping Rain Water">

- water[i] = min(max height to the left including i, max to the right including i) - h[i]; sum over i.
- Prefix maxima: two sweeps + one combine. O(n) time, O(n) space.
- Two pointers: settle the side with the lower bar using that side's running max. O(n) time, O(1) space.
- Why safe: the other side already has a wall at least as tall as this side's max.
- Not Container: every bar matters, the answer is a sum, the pool can be deeper than the end walls.

</CheatCard>
